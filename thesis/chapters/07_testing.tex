\chapter{Testing and Evaluation}
\label{chap:testing}

This chapter presents the results of the developed models and evaluates how they perform at the different resolution levels. In addition to the normal test results, Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++, and an external test dataset were also used to better understand the models behaviour.

\section{Experimental Setup}

To keep the evaluation reliable, the same patient-based dataset split described in Section~\ref{sec:dataset-description} was used for all experiments. The NLM dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets using a 70/15/15 ratio with a fixed random seed of 42. This resulted in 4186 training images, 899 validation images, and 953 test images. The split was based on patients, so images from the same patient appear in only one split. 

The test set contains 750 infected images and 203 uninfected images. The number of infected samples is therefore considerably higher than the number of uninfected samples. This class imbalance is important when interpreting the results, especially sensitivity and specificity, since a high overall accuracy alone does not show how well both classes are recognised.

A total of eight main experiments were performed. ResNet50 was evaluated at all four resolution conditions (HR, LR1, LR2, and LR3), while ResNet18 and MobileNetV3-Small were evaluated only at HR and LR3. After the ResNet50 experiments showed that LR1 remained very close to the HR condition, the intermediate resolution levels were not repeated for the other architectures. Instead, HR and LR3 were selected to represent the two ends of the investigated resolution range. The exact image resolutions and preprocessing steps for HR, LR1, LR2, and LR3 are shown and explained in Section~\ref{sec:data-preprocessing}.

The training settings used for the eight main experiments are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:experimental_setup}. The same configuration was used for all eight baseline experiments, with 15 epochs, a batch size of 8, a learning rate of \(0.0001\), and CrossEntropyLoss. MobileNetV3-Small at LR3 was additionally optimised, as described in Section~\ref{sec:pipeline-architecture}. The final configuration used 30 epochs, a batch size of 16, a learning rate of \(0.00003\), and a weighted CrossEntropyLoss with class weights of \([2.0,1.0]\).

For the final evaluation, the saved \textbf{best\_model.pth} checkpoint from each experiment was loaded and evaluated on the test split. The checkpoint was selected during training based on the validation accuracy, as described in Chapter~\ref{chap:concept-development}. During testing, the model was set to evaluation mode and its parameters were not changed.

The reported evaluation metrics are accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, and ROC-AUC. The definitions and meaning of these metrics were already introduced in Section~\ref{sec:deep_learning_fundamentals}. Because the model is intended for screening, sensitivity is especially important. A false negative means that an infected image is classified as uninfected, which should be avoided in a screening system. Specificity is considered together with sensitivity to show whether good detection of infected images is achieved without incorrectly classifying too many uninfected images as infected.

All three models achieved very high results at full resolution, and the differences between them were small. ResNet50 reached an accuracy of 0.9958 and misclassified four of the 953 test images. ResNet18 and MobileNetV3-Small both reached an accuracy of 0.9969. Both models correctly classified all 750 infected images and therefore achieved a sensitivity of 1.0000. Each model incorrectly classified three of the 203 uninfected images as infected, resulting in a specificity of 0.9852.

The absence of false negatives in ResNet18 and MobileNetV3-Small is important for screening, as zero infected images were missed. In general, the differences between the three architectures are very small and are based on a few images. The HR results show that all three architectures perform similarly well under the normal resolution condition.

As can be seen from the results table and the confusion matrices, the overall performance stayed good under the first reduced resolution, from HR to LR1. At LR2, the accuracy decreased to 0.9843, while sensitivity remained high at 0.9960. The clearer change was seen in specificity, which decreased from 0.9901 at HR to 0.9409 at LR2. In LR2, 12 uninfected images were classified as infected and three infected images were classified as uninfected.

LR3 showed the strongest reduction in performance. Accuracy decreased to 0.9286 and specificity dropped to 0.7389. The model incorrectly classified 53 uninfected images as infected and missed 15 infected images. Sensitivity still remained relatively high at 0.9800, but the number of false positives increased clearly compared with previous resolutions.

In general, ResNet50 remained very stable between HR and LR1. The performance started to decrease more clearly at LR2 and especially at LR3. The largest change across the resolution levels was seen in specificity, while sensitivity stayed high across all four conditions.

Since MobileNetV3-Small was the architecture considered for lightweight deployment, a two-stage optimisation was investigated in the following section.

\section{MobileNet Optimisation at LR3}

Considering that MobileNetV3-Small was selected as a lightweight architecture for future deployment, its performance at LR3 was investigated further. The baseline model showed lower performance than the two ResNet architectures, especially in specificity. Therefore, two optimisation stages were performed.

In the first optimisation stage, the learning rate was reduced from 0.0001 to 0.00003, the batch size was increased from 8 to 16, and the number of training epochs was increased from 15 to 30. In the second stage, the same settings were kept and class weights of \([2.0,1.0]\) were added to the CrossEntropyLoss.

The results of the three stages are shown in Table~\ref{tab:mobilenet_lr3_optimisation}.

The specificity decreased slightly from 0.6897 to 0.6700, and the number of false positives increased from 63 to 67.

Because of the class imbalance in the dataset, class weights were added in the second optimisation stage. This improved the accuracy to 0.9276. The biggest improvement was seen in specificity, which increased to 0.7931 as false positives dropped to 42. Meanwhile, sensitivity reached 0.9640.

The second optimisation stage gave a more balanced result between sensitivity and specificity. It also achieved the highest accuracy, precision, and F1-score of the three MobileNetV3-Small LR3 experiments. Although the sensitivity was lower than in the first optimisation stage, it was still higher than the baseline result.

\section{External Validation}

To investigate whether the models may have relied on edge characteristics in the NLM dataset, an external test was performed on a new dataset that the models had never seen before. This dataset is from AIR Uganda. It contains 1182 malaria-positive images and has different image characteristics compared with the NLM dataset. No uninfected images are involved in the test.

The Uganda images were only used for external testing with already trained checkpoints. No additional training or optimisation was performed on the Uganda images.

The findings in the external test indicate that the activation around image boundaries observed in the Grad-CAM analysis does not automatically mean that the models fail on images with different characteristics. Since this experiment tests the models only on positive samples, nothing can be said about specificity or how well the models predict uninfected images.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

After evaluating all results, it is clear that all three architectures performed very similarly at full resolution (HR). The achieved accuracies from the models were around 0.996 and the differences between them were based on only a few wrong predictions. The effect of reducing the resolution became more visible at LR2 and significantly at LR3. For ResNet50, sensitivity remained high across all resolution levels, while specificity decreased sharply when the resolution decreased. To be more precise, specificity changed from 0.9901 at HR to 0.9852 at LR1. At LR2 it decreased even more to 0.9409. At LR3, it reached its lowest 0.7389. These observed metrics show that the reduced resolution caused a number of uninfected images to be misclassified as infected.

At LR3, the differences between the architectures became more clear. ResNet50 reached an accuracy of 0.9286, while the baseline MobileNetV3-Small reached 0.8930. After the two optimisation stages, MobileNetV3-Small did improve in terms of accuracy, which reached 0.9276.  This optimisation made the MobileNetV3-Small performance very close to ResNet50, which further supports the argument for selecting MobileNetV3-Small as the lightweight architecture to be considered for future deployment.

This MobileNet optimisation also showed a clear trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. The first optimisation improved the sensitivity by reaching 0.9920, but specificity was only 0.6700. The second optimisation stage compromised slightly on sensitivity (0.9640) to increase specificity to 0.7931. This represents a more balanced result between the two classes. 

For a screening application, which is the end goal of this overall project, a high sensitivity is very important to prevent a high number of false-negative case. If the deployment relies also on a second test that validates the model prediction, then the first optimisation stage could therefore also be considered despite its lower specificity. Stage 2 was selected by comparing the test-set metrics, so its reported performance may be slightly optimistic. The Uganda dataset was not used for this selection.

The Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM++ visualisations indicated that the models may not always focus only on regions inside the microscope field, but also on the edges. In several Grad-CAM outputs, activation was visible in the surrounding background. This raised the question whether dataset-specific image characteristics may have played a role in some predictions. This issue was investigated with a test using an external dataset from AIR Uganda, which showed that almost all models still score very well on images with different characteristics than NLM images. In the external test, ResNet50 reached a sensitivity of 1.0000 at HR and 0.9848 at LR3. The optimised MobileNetV3-Small also reached a high sensitivity of 0.9560. On the other hand, ResNet18 at LR3 performed badly and reached a sensitivity of only 0.8232. This shows that robustness was not the same for all architectures.  

The strong external results suggest that the models did not rely only on the NLM image borders for their predictions.

The LR3 experiments are very relevant for the ocular adapter part of the overall project. These results show that effective classification was still possible even after a strong resolution reduction. This is considered positive for a future system where image quality may be low due to limited resources. Yet, LR3 was created by downsampling and does not represent real images captured directly through the actual ocular adapter, which means a real field performance cannot be determined here.

The external Uganda dataset also has some limitations. It contains only malaria-positive images. That said, specificity and false-positive behaviour could not be evaluated. 

It's also worth mentioning that not all trained models were evaluated on the external Uganda dataset. ResNet18-HR, MobileNetV3-Small-HR, ResNet50-LR1, and ResNet50-LR2 were not included in the external test. In addition, the system developed in this thesis has not yet been evaluated on real field images from Ethiopia or under clinical conditions. These points should be investigated in future work.
