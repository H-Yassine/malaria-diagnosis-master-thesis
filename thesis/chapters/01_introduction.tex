\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

To this day, malaria continues to pose a major global health challenge. The World Health Organisation (WHO) reports that in 2020, about 241 million people were infected with malaria worldwide, and approximately 627 000 lives were lost to the disease\cite{WHO2021Report}. Among all regions, Africa bears by far the heaviest burden, accounting for the vast majority of cases and deaths\cite{WHO2021Report}.
Early diagnosis is especially important because malaria can develop into a severe and potentially life-threatening disease if treatment is delayed. Prompt treatment within 24 hours after the onset of fever is recommended and plays an important role in reducing malaria-related mortality, particularly among children under five years of age\cite{Maturana2022}.
Although malaria vaccines are available, vaccination alone does not provide complete protection and therefore does not replace the need for reliable diagnosis and treatment\cite{Maturana2022}. Considering these limitations, this work addresses the need for more efficient diagnostic approaches.

Although microscopic examination of blood smears is still considered the gold standard for malaria diagnosis, it has several limitations. The examination consumes time and requires trained and experienced personnel. The result can also depend on the microscopist, which can lead to differences between observations. This becomes particularly challenging in malaria-endemic regions where trained personnel and laboratory resources may be limited \cite{Maturana2022,Rajaraman2019}. Other diagnostic methods, such as rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR), are available, but they also have limitations and therefore do not completely replace microscopy \cite{Maturana2022}.
These limitations have increased the interest in computer-aided and automated analysis of blood smear images. Earlier approaches often relied on manually designed image features, which can depend strongly on the dataset and imaging conditions. Deep learning, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs), provides another approach by learning relevant features directly from image data instead of defining them manually \cite{Rajaraman2019,Rajaraman2018}. This makes CNNs a promising approach for supporting and automating malaria diagnosis. In addition, automated image analysis can be combined with smartphone-based image acquisition, which offers a practical and relatively low-cost solution for settings with limited resources \cite{Maturana2022}. This idea also fits the overall approach of this project, where a smartphone and ocular adapter are used to digitise microscope images for further AI-based analysis.

This thesis is part of a three-part Master's project at the University of Applied Sciences and Arts Hildesheim/Holzminden/Göttingen (HAWK) that focuses on the development of a practical AI-based approach for malaria diagnosis from Giemsa-stained thick blood smears. The project is conducted in cooperation with the Ethiopian Artificial Intelligence Institute (EAII) in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, and CarByte Engineering GmbH in Germany. CarByte provides supervision during the development of the three individual projects. The AI algorithms are developed in Python so that they can also be used and further developed by EAII.
The overall project is divided into three connected Master's projects. Nabil Laaroussi focuses on the development of an ocular adapter that allows microscope images to be captured using a smartphone. Zaineb Lerhmame works on a resolution-adaptive simulation database to study how reduced image resolution affects the diagnostic performance. This thesis focuses on the development and evaluation of the AI-based diagnostic method, with particular attention to its performance under reduced image resolution relevant to the future ocular-adapter setup. Together, these parts contribute to a prototype in which images from an analog microscope can be digitised using a smartphone and then processed by a PC-based diagnostic tool. The current project focuses on the development of a first prototype. Further improvements, especially regarding the robustness required for field use, can be addressed in future work.

The objective of this thesis is to develop and evaluate a deep learning-based method for automatic malaria prescreening from Giemsa-stained thick blood smear images. A particular focus is placed on the influence of reduced image resolution, since the diagnostic method should also work with the image quality provided by the smartphone-based ocular adapter. The developed approach is evaluated at different image resolutions and benchmarked against existing approaches to assess its suitability for the overall diagnostic system.

To achieve the objective of this thesis, the work is divided into several main steps. First, the existing literature on AI-based malaria diagnosis is reviewed and the relevant fundamentals of deep learning are presented. The next step focuses on collecting suitable image data from available malaria databases and preparing the data for further processing. Based on the findings from the literature, suitable neural network architectures are then selected, implemented, tested, and optimised. This includes investigating the network architecture and relevant hyperparameters. Finally, the developed approach is benchmarked against existing methods from the literature, followed by an evaluation and documentation of the results.

This thesis focuses on the development and evaluation of the AI-based diagnostic method and does not cover the development of the hardware used for image acquisition. The design and construction of the smartphone-based ocular adapter are part of a separate thesis by Nabil Laaroussi. Similarly, the development of the resolution-adaptive simulation database is carried out by Zaineb Lerhmame and is not part of this work.
The diagnostic task in this thesis is limited to the binary classification of Giemsa-stained thick blood smear images as infected or uninfected. Parasite localisation, quantification of parasitemia, species-level classification, and the analysis of thin blood smears are not covered. Furthermore, clinical validation and field testing in Ethiopian health centres are outside the scope of this thesis.
